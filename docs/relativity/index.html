No-go theorems for extended bodies

Born (1909) proposed a definition of the rigid body suited to the theory of relativity. Ehrenfest (1909) showed that a body rigid in this sense cannot be set into uniform rotation; Herglotz and Noether (1910) showed that the definition leaves a body only three degrees of freedom; and Laue (1911) argued that, since no action propagates faster than light, no body can have a finite number of degrees of freedom.

Uniform rotation of rigid bodies and the theory of relativity
Paul Ehrenfest (1880–1933). Physikalische Zeitschrift 10 (1909): 918
A one-page note arguing that relativistic rigidity cannot be maintained for a cylinder set into uniform rotation: its periphery should contract while its radius should not. The origin of what became known as the Ehrenfest paradox.

The Danish relativity debate, 1912–1917

H. M. Hansen introduced the special theory of relativity to Danish physicists and mathematicians in a lecture of 1912. In 1915 the philosopher Kristian Kroman attacked the theory in Fysisk Tidsskrift: if each of two observers correctly measures the other's rod as the shorter, there are two truths, and »Sandheden er ikke dobbelt«. Helge Holst, C. E. Walsøe and Hansen replied in 1916, Holst again in 1917, and Kroman closed the debate the same year. The texts are also part of the Danish Texts collection.

Relativitetsprincipet
The principle of relativity
H. M. Hansen (1886–1956). Fysisk Tidsskrift 10 (1911–12): 251–281
A lecture to a joint meeting of Fysisk Forening and Mathematisk Forening, 11 April 1912: the first real introduction of special relativity to a Danish audience, and the lecture that provoked Kroman three years later. Hansen was Niels Bohr's collaborator.

Relativitetsprincipet
The principle of relativity
Kristian Kroman (1846–1925). Fysisk Tidsskrift 14 (1915–16): 1–30
Kroman's attack on special relativity. If two observers each correctly measure the other's rod as the shorter, then there are two truths, and »Sandheden er ikke dobbelt«: the theory contradicts the principle of non-contradiction and must be rejected. Høffding answers the argument by name in »Relation som Kategori« (1921).

Relativistic positivism, 1912–1921

Joseph Petzoldt, a follower of Mach and Avenarius, called his philosophy »relativistic positivism« and read the theory of relativity as its confirmation in physics: nothing is absolute.

Relativity and the a priori, 1920–1922

Reichenbach (1920) and Cassirer (1921) asked what the theory of relativity leaves of the Kantian a priori. Schlick answered Cassirer's book in Kant-Studien (1921) with an empiricist reading of the new physics against Cassirer's critical one, and Reichenbach surveyed the state of the philosophical discussion in Logos (1922).
