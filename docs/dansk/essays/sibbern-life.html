Hans Halvorson

The Doctor's Son Called Gabrielis

The catalog entry for F.C. Sibbern gives the professor: the Copenhagen chair held from 1813 to 1870, the friend of Poul Martin Møller, the teacher whose lectures Kierkegaard attended. A biographical essay by Henrik Schovsbo, published on the Royal Danish Library’s text portal as the introduction to a scholarly edition of Sibbern’s two Gabrielis novels, fills in the life that produced him — and it turns out that several things this catalog already holds, not least the novels’ odd shared title, only make sense once that life is known.1

A doctor’s son named for his father

Sibbern was born in Copenhagen, son of a physician, Frederik Gabriel Gottlieb Sibbern, and his wife Charlotte Amalia Frederica Lovisa, née Wilcken. Both parents were of Holstein stock and spoke German at home — unremarkable in a Copenhagen that was then capital of a joint Danish-Norwegian-German monarchy — and both belonged to the German-inflected pietist Brødremenighed, the Moravian Brethren, the same fellowship Kierkegaard’s own family felt bound to. “Doctor Gabriel’s son” is, in Latin, Gabrielis — the genitive Schovsbo gives as the source of the pen name and title character of the two novels this catalog holds, Efterladte Breve af Gabrielis (1826) and Udaf Gabrielis’s Breve til og fra Hjemmet (1850). The boy attended Copenhagen’s German St. Petri school until his father’s early death, after which the gifted child was taken in at Herlufsholm, the boarding school near Næstved. Where German language and culture had shaped him until then, Schovsbo writes, he became Danish there.

Three gold medals and a law degree

He was a model student: matriculated in 1802, took his law degree (juridisk kandidat) in 1810, and the doctorate the following year on a dissertation in legal philosophy — the De principiis philosophicis disciplina juris this catalog’s bibliography already lists under 1811. He also read mathematics and natural science alongside law, and won the university’s gold medal in philosophical questions three times over.

Walking to Hegel, and to Goethe

A dissertation earned a young Danish scholar the customary dannelsesrejse, an educational tour abroad, and Sibbern’s turned out to decide the rest of his career. While he was still travelling, the newly founded university at Christiania — Oslo — offered him its chair in law; he declined it. He was frugal and Denmark was poor, so he covered the tour on foot, walking German roads from philosopher to philosopher. He sought out Hegel, at that point a gymnasium rector in Nuremberg rather than the dominant figure he would become, and reached Goethe as well: finding the poet away from Weimar and on holiday in Karlsbad, Sibbern walked on into what is now the Czech Republic and spent an afternoon with him in the spa gardens. Of lasting importance was a second meeting with Heinrich Steffens, whom Sibbern had first heard, without much effect at the time, at Steffens’s celebrated lectures at Copenhagen’s Ehlers Kollegium in the winter of 1802–03 — sitting, that first time, alongside Adam Oehlenschläger, N.F.S. Grundtvig, St. St. Blicher, and Schack von Staffeldt. But it was Schelling, met on this same journey, whose romantic identification of spirit and matter left the deepest and most permanent mark, together with the broader romantic conviction that art — poetry above all — was a source of knowledge on a footing with natural observation and rational speculation. Both convictions run through Sibbern’s own later work in psychology and aesthetics alike.

A rival twice over

Sibbern was still abroad when Copenhagen called him home in 1813 to its new chair in philosophy. The ageing poet Jens Baggesen, who felt exiled to the provinces in his own professorship at Kiel, wanted the position badly and lost it to a twenty-eight-year-old he privately regarded as little more than a favored schoolboy. It was not the only thing Baggesen lost to him: Sibbern had already formed the kind of chaste, intense spiritual attachment the age prized to one of its most celebrated women, Sophie Ørsted (1782–1818) — Adam Oehlenschläger’s sister, married to the jurist and statesman Anders Sandøe Ørsted — and it was Baggesen who, after Sibbern, succeeded to that same devoted role. Sophie Ørsted died in 1818 with the manuscript of Efterladte Breve af Gabrielis nearly finished; Sibbern, by his biographer Jens Himmelstrup’s account, had read much of it aloud to her before her death, wanting her to have answered the questions his infatuation had raised. The novel did not appear for another eight years, and its contemporary readers took it, not entirely wrongly, as a roman à clef about her.

Fifty-seven years in the chair

Sibbern held the Copenhagen professorship until 1870, and Schovsbo counts fifty-seven years’ worth of students, Poul Martin Møller and Kierkegaard among them, shaped by his lectures and his introductory textbooks. He built the philosophy curriculum up largely from nothing in his first years, teaching elementary logic while also detaching psychology from earlier speculative treatments and grounding it empirically — the discipline whose principal statement, Menneskets aandelige Natur og Væsen (1819) and its sequel, dated 1820 in Schovsbo’s account against the 1828 this catalog’s bibliography gives for the same Psychologisk Pathologie, went through reprintings as late as 1885.2 Over the following decades Sibbern’s own position settled into an optimistic conviction that an “all-intelligent” ideal-religious power, at once personal deity and world-governance, underlies the cosmos and remains open to human understanding — a Christianity increasingly filtered through romantic and speculative categories, shedding whatever would not go into concepts. It was this milieu that formed Kierkegaard, who took a great deal from it and then broke just as violently with its harmony-seeking religious temper. Sibbern was more than his student’s teacher: he was also his regular walking companion in later years, and sat as an examiner on his dissertation.

Remembered for the wrong half

Schovsbo’s own verdict, offered to introduce an edition of the novels rather than the philosophy, is blunt: the philosophical and psychological work that occupied most of Sibbern’s working life is now largely forgotten, and he survives in Danish letters chiefly as the author of the two Gabrielis books — the very books this catalog’s bibliography files as incidental to the philosophy, and which began, on Schovsbo’s telling, as one professor’s way of answering a question a woman he loved never got to ask.
